# Build your monthly money report from statements

For one person in India who wants a monthly spending report with advice, the best route today is a small custom pipeline you write yourself. It reads the **statements your banks and card issuers already send or let you download** (emailed PDFs and net-banking CSV/XLS files), sorts transactions with rules first and an LLM only for the leftovers, and has an LLM write the "what went well / where to improve" text from numbers your code has already worked out. The fancier routes are mostly closed to individuals. The **Account Aggregator (AA) network only admits regulated financial companies** ([RBI Master Direction](https://rbi.org.in/scripts/BS_ViewMasDirections.aspx?id=10598)) and appears not to carry credit-card data ([Mojek help](https://mojekmoney.help.center/article/1079-how-do-you-fetch-my-credit-card-related-data.md)). Indian bank APIs serve businesses, not retail customers ([ICICI](https://www.icici.bank.in/blogs/current-account/api-banking-icici)). No self-hosted finance app connects directly to Indian banks either. The open-source scene is lively but scattered. **PennyWise AI** (607 stars, 57 Indian banks) leads for SMS tracking on Android ([GitHub](https://github.com/sarim2000/pennywiseai-tracker)), and **Firefly III, Actual Budget, Sure and Beancount** are the mature general-purpose ledgers. For Indian banks, though, all of them need importers that the community writes for each bank. The legal side is reassuring for a tool only you use: India's data protection law **excludes purely personal processing** ([DPDP Wiki](https://dpdp.myndsolution.com/wiki/guides/who-does-the-act-apply-to/)), and RBI rules apply only if you become a product. Your real risks are security (bank PDF passwords, Gmail access, an LLM inventing numbers) and maintenance (banks change their statement layouts). Because you want a monthly report rather than a live dashboard, you can skip the hardest part, real-time SMS capture, and build on the most complete and checkable data source there is: the monthly statement.

## Four data routes work for individuals; two famous ones do not

Every Indian expense tracker, open source or commercial, has to solve the same first problem: getting transactions out of your banks. There are six candidate routes, and they differ sharply in completeness, effort and whether an individual can use them at all.

| Route | Can an individual use it? | Covers cards? | Real-time? | Main catch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Emailed PDF statements (Gmail) | Yes | Yes | No (monthly) | Password-protected; layout differs by bank |
| Transaction alert emails | Yes | Yes | Yes | Not every bank emails every transaction |
| Bank SMS alerts | Android yes; iPhone only via Shortcuts | Yes | Yes | Gaps for small UPI payments at some banks |
| Net-banking CSV/XLS download | Yes, manually | Varies | No | No documented column format |
| UPI app exports (GPay, PhonePe) | Yes | No | No | Only that app's payments; best for enrichment |
| Account Aggregator / bank APIs | **No** | AA: apparently not | n/a | Only regulated entities and businesses |

**Emailed statements are the broadest single source**, covering both bank accounts and credit cards. Nearly all come as password-protected PDFs, and the password rules are a mess. For HDFC credit cards the usual claim is the first four letters of your name in capitals plus the last four card digits ([ClearTax](https://cleartax.in/s/hdfc-bank-statement-password)). ICICI is often given as date of birth in DDMMYYYY form ([EaseMoney](https://easemoney.in/icici-credit-card-statement-password/)), and SBI as name-four plus DDMM ([ClearTax SBI](https://cleartax.in/s/sbi-statement-password)). Other sources contradict each of these, and a bank's savings-account and card statements can use different passwords ([Univik](https://univik.com/blog/credit-card-statement-pdf-password.html)). The practical lesson: store each account's password in your config (or keychain) rather than trying to guess it. Once a PDF is unlocked, Python tools such as **pikepdf/qpdf** (decryption) and **pdfplumber** or **Camelot** (table extraction) handle the text. Bank tables are usually borderless, which suits pdfplumber or Camelot's "stream" mode better than its "lattice" mode ([comparison](https://invoicedataextraction.com/blog/python-pdf-table-extraction-invoices)).

Getting at Gmail programmatically is harder than it looks. Gmail's read permission is a "restricted" scope. A public app needs Google verification plus a yearly CASA security audit ([Nango](https://nango.dev/docs/integrations/google-security-review)), costing roughly **$540–$1,000+ a year** and taking 2–8 weeks ([Unipile](https://www.unipile.com/integrating-google-oauth-2-0-user-authentication-into-your-app/)). A personal app can skip that, but in "Testing" mode its login token **expires every 7 days** ([Nango Gmail docs](https://www.nango.dev/docs/integrations/all/google-mail)). That is why self-hosters overwhelmingly use **IMAP with an app password** instead: a 16-character password Google gives you once 2-Step Verification is on. It still works in 2026 ([Nylas guide](https://cli.nylas.com/guides/gmail-app-password-setup)), never expires, and needs no Google review. The trade-off is that it grants access to your whole mailbox, so guard it carefully.

**Alerts (email and SMS) are fast but incomplete.** HDFC is the clearest example: since 25 June 2024 it no longer sends SMS for UPI payments up to ₹100 sent or ₹500 received, but it still emails every UPI transaction ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/industry/banks/story/hdfc-bank-to-stop-sms-alerts-for-upi-payments-up-to-rs-100-email-notifications-to-continue-431258-2024-05-29)). Other users report that their bank does not email every transaction ([Reddit](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1pje5tz/snapshots/2025-12-11T02%3A21%3A17.901903Z)). So neither channel alone is complete, and alerts are best treated as an early preview that the statement later confirms. On Android, Play Store apps may read SMS only if they are your default SMS app or win a case-by-case exception ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10208820?hl=en)). This is why personal projects use sideloaded forwarders such as the MIT-licensed **android_income_sms_gateway_webhook**, which posts each incoming SMS to a URL you choose ([listing](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fbogkonstantin%2Fandroid_income_sms_gateway_webhook)). Its README now says it is no longer actively developed. **iPhones cannot let any app read SMS**. The only workaround is a Shortcuts automation that fires when a message contains words like "debited" or "spent" ([Pocket Clear](https://pocketclear.app/blog/auto-log-apple-pay-bank-sms-iphone-shortcuts.html)), and it breaks whenever a bank rewords its texts.

**Net-banking downloads are the most structured manual route.** HDFC, for example, offers a "Delimited" (CSV-like) download of up to five years of history ([Expense_manager](https://github.com/Anirudh1905/Expense_manager)). But no bank documents its column layout, so you write a small parser for each bank from real samples. **UPI app exports are only a supplement.** Google Pay India offers an in-app statement covering only Google Pay transactions ([Google Pay Help](https://support.google.com/pay/india/answer/7430307?hl=en-IN)). The same debit already appears in your bank statement, so these exports are useful mainly for adding payee names and notes.

The two routes that sound most promising are closed. Account Aggregator is RBI's consent-based data-sharing network. Data can only be received by a "Financial Information User" (FIU), and an FIU must be regulated by RBI, SEBI, IRDAI or PFRDA ([Sahamati](https://sahamati.org.in/fiu/)). Even the sandbox at providers like Setu asks for a company PAN and GSTIN ([Setu](https://support.setu.co/support/solutions/articles/81000412878-how-does-one-generate-sandbox-credentials-)). Sahamati's list of live account types shows savings, current and deposit accounts, not credit cards ([Sahamati](https://sahamati.org.in/account-types-activated-on-aas/)). Consumer AA apps like Finvu and Onemoney manage consent but offer no export, because the AA passes data through without storing it ([Finvu](https://apps.apple.com/in/app/finvu/id6499302887)). Whether a credit-card data type has gone live by late 2026 could not be confirmed, but no evidence suggests it has.

## India's open-source trackers are SMS-first and mostly one-person projects

The most active open-source work in India is on Android SMS trackers, and one project clearly leads. **PennyWise AI** ([GitHub](https://github.com/sarim2000/pennywiseai-tracker)) is a Kotlin app with **607 stars, 1,419 commits, AGPL-3.0, on F-Droid**, supporting 57 Indian banks among 171 worldwide. It parses SMS with per-bank patterns kept in a separate `parser-core` module, runs a small AI model entirely on the phone (Qwen 2.5, about 1.5 GB), and recently added tracking from bank-app notifications ([F-Droid](https://f-droid.org/ko/packages/com.pennywiseai.tracker)). Two caveats matter for you. First, **PDF statement import and CSV export sit behind an optional paid "Pro" tier**, so getting your data out cleanly is not free. Second, its open issues show the usual problems: missed transactions, balance mismatches, and requests for refund linking and support for unlisted banks ([issues](https://github.com/sarim2000/pennywiseai-tracker/issues)). **Cashiro** ([GitHub](https://github.com/ritesh-kanwar/Cashiro)) is a fork-like app openly inspired by PennyWise (204 stars, AGPL-3.0, 40+ Indian banks), but it carries 94 open issues. For a reusable library, **transaction-sms-parser** (TypeScript, MIT) turns one SMS into a structured account/amount/merchant object for about a dozen Indian banks and cards ([GitHub](https://github.com/saurabhgupta050890/transaction-sms-parser)). Its npm release has not been updated since **July 2024** ([socket.dev](https://socket.dev/npm/package/transaction-sms-parser)).

Statement parsing is far more fragmented. The one mature, widely used parser is **casparser** (MIT, 239 stars), which reads mutual-fund and demat Consolidated Account Statements from CAMS, KFintech, NSDL and CDSL ([GitHub](https://github.com/codereverser/casparser)). If you want investments in your report, adopt it directly. For bank and card statements there is no dominant library, only small repos with fewer than 20 stars each. The most useful is **akhilnarang/bank-statement-parser** (MIT), which covers HDFC, ICICI, IDFC FIRST, IndusInd, Kotak, SBI, Slice and Union Bank. It also runs a **reconciliation check** (opening balance + credits − debits must equal closing balance, to the paisa), which is the single best guard against a parser silently dropping rows ([GitHub](https://github.com/akhilnarang/bank-statement-parser)). Its test folder is empty, though. For cards there are **xaneem/hdfc-credit-card-statement-parser** (MIT, "built for personal use") ([GitHub](https://github.com/xaneem/hdfc-credit-card-statement-parser)) and **tanumay-deb/credit-card-spend-intelligence**, which fetches card PDFs from Gmail and has HDFC/ICICI/SBI parsers ([GitHub](https://github.com/tanumay-deb/credit-card-spend-intelligence)). Axis card coverage is notably thin.

Gmail-based extractors are almost all **2025–26 hobby projects with zero or a handful of stars**, but together they show a consistent design. The most complete is **medhruv7/Expense-Manager** ([GitHub](https://github.com/medhruv7/Expense-Manager)). It reads Gmail over IMAP with an app password every 15 minutes, takes SMS from an Android forwarder, and parses with per-bank regex (text-matching patterns), sending only failures to a local or cloud LLM. It removes duplicates when the same payment arrives by SMS and email, matching on the UPI reference, and it treats a matching debit and credit within 30 minutes as a transfer between your own accounts. Others follow similar ideas: **androdev700/money-tracker** sends unparseable mail to a review queue ([GitHub](https://github.com/androdev700/money-tracker)), and **okpalindrome/xpens** keys every record on the Gmail message ID so a re-sync never duplicates anything ([GitHub](https://github.com/okpalindrome/xpens)). On the self-hosted side, the most-starred India-authored project is **paisa** (3.2k stars, AGPL-3.0), a ledger-based web app ([GitHub](https://github.com/ananthakumaran/paisa)). Its Indian-bank import templates could not be verified for this report.

The lesson across all of these is that **parsers break, and the tools survive by making breakage visible**. Projects report the same pitfalls again and again. Reprinted PDFs fail verification ([casparser](https://github.com/codereverser/casparser)). SMS sometimes lacks enough detail ([punchagan](https://github.com/punchagan/expense-tracker)). Merchant names are the weakest part of extraction ([umber](https://github.com/DeepakSilaych/umber/releases/tag/v0.1.0)). Android 13+ blocks SMS permission for sideloaded apps until you change a "restricted settings" toggle ([medhruv7](https://github.com/medhruv7/Expense-Manager)). The good projects answer with reconciliation checks, unique IDs, and a queue of uncertain items for you to review.

## Self-hosted finance apps give you a ledger, not Indian bank connections

If you would rather adopt an existing app than build a store and UI yourself, five candidates are realistic. All handle INR fine. The India gap is bank connectivity, not currency.

| App | What it is | Indian-bank data | AI / advice | Fit for "monthly report + advice" |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Firefly III** ([docs](https://docs.firefly-iii.org/tutorials/firefly-iii/importing-data/)) | Mature double-entry web app, strong rules + REST API, very active releases | CSV via Data Importer; community Axis/SBI/ICICI PDF helper ([anil-dewani](https://github.imc.re/anil-dewani/firefly-import-helper)) | External categorizers ([Firefly-III-LLM-Categorizer](https://1clonegithubbyxjn.1934672944.workers.dev/constLiakos/Firefly-III-LLM-Categorizer)) | Good store; heavy for one person; report must be built on its API |
| **Actual Budget** ([overview](https://selfhosttools.pages.dev/tools/actual-budget)) | Local-first envelope budgeting, SQLite | CSV import only | **actual-ai** (MIT, ~538 stars) LLM categorizer ([GitHub](https://github.com/sakowicz/actual-ai)); MCP servers ([actual-mcp](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@s-stefanov/actual-mcp/tree)) | Best if you want budgeting envelopes |
| **Sure** ([GitHub](https://github.com/we-promise/sure)) | Community fork of the now-dead Maybe, AGPLv3, ~10.7k stars | Import; no Indian bank sync | Built-in AI assistant; Anthropic native since v0.7.2 ([release](https://newreleases.io/project/github/we-promise/sure/release/v0.7.1)) | Closest to "CRED-like" out of the box, but 448 open issues and alpha churn |
| **Beancount + Fava** ([docs](https://beancount.github.io/docs/importing_external_data/)) | Plain-text double-entry ledger, Python importers | Indian importers for SBI, BOI, Paytm, HSBC, ICICI ([Awesome Beancount](https://beancount.io/awesome-beancount)); HDFC gist ([Gist](https://gist.github.com/BharatKalluri/17d14353a83a3e58ffb5639e7b00348d)) | smart_importer learns from your corrections ([beancount.io](https://beancount.io/docs/Solutions/using-llms-to-automate-and-enhance-bookkeeping-with-beancount)) | Most control for a Python developer; double-entry learning curve |
| **PennyWise AI** ([GitHub](https://github.com/sarim2000/pennywiseai-tracker)) | On-phone Android SMS tracker | 57 Indian banks via SMS | On-device small model | Great capture, not a monthly-advice engine; export is Pro |

A few facts shape the choice. **Maybe Finance stopped maintaining its open-source app in July 2025** with a final v0.6.0 ([Lemmy thread](https://deddit.petersanchez.com/g/selfhosted@lemmy.world/p/Y835h27836bM2q539T-Maybe-financial-tracker-shuts-down-releasing-a-f)). Sure is the living continuation, shipping monthly releases, but much of its recent work is UI, and its newest builds are alphas ([v0.7.6-alpha.3](https://newreleases.io/project/github/we-promise/sure/release/v0.7.6-alpha.3)). **Firefly III releases constantly** (v6.6.6 by July 2026) ([newreleases](https://newreleases.io/project/github/firefly-iii/firefly-iii/release/develop-20260701.1)). Indian users already run it at home: one March 2026 build parses Indian bank SMS into Firefly and uses the UPI reference to avoid double-counting transfers ([blog](https://vickytechlove.blogspot.com)). YNAB costs close to ₹10,000 a year in India and has no Indian bank sync, which pushes people toward Actual ([r/ynab](https://lr.eu.psf.lt/r/ynab/comments/1e3v11g/bidding_goodbye_fiver_years_of_ynab/ldbiej5/?context=3)). The maintenance burden is real everywhere. A GnuCash user who published Indian bank import scripts in April 2026 had to update them in July after **SBI changed its export format** ([gnucash-user](https://lists.gnucash.org/pipermail/gnucash-user/2026-July/121136.html)).

The honest reading: **adopting any of these saves you the database and the screens, but not the hard work.** You still write or borrow the Indian-bank importers, and none of them ships a well-documented monthly "what went well / where to improve" generator. Since you explicitly don't care about a dashboard, the main thing these apps give you is the part you need least.

## LLMs categorize best as a last resort and advise best as narrators

**Categorization works best in layers, with the LLM last.** Studies of general-purpose LLMs sorting transactions into many categories with no examples report modest accuracy. Zero-shot GPT-4o scored **60.4% versus 73.4% for a fine-tuned model** on small-business bank data ([arXiv 2508.05425](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.05425)), and the best general model plateaued around **66%** on charts of accounts with up to 281 categories ([Digits](https://digits.com/_assets/downloads/beyond-the-hype-evaluating-llms-vs-digits-agl.pdf)). Matching against your own past, confirmed labels is strong for recurring spending ([arXiv 2506.09234](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.09234)). Personal setups with a small category list and that kind of feedback loop self-report about **95% automation** ([beancount.io forum](https://beancount.io/forum/t/finally-got-95-automated-expense-categorization-working-with-beancount-llms/93)). That figure is anecdotal, but it fits the pattern.

India helps here, because UPI narrations are partly standardized. NPCI's 2018 circular fixed a common structure: rail, a 12-digit UPI reference, payee name, bank code and remark ([NPCI Circular 43](https://www.npci.org.in/PDF/npci/upi/circular/2018/UPI%20-%20Circular%20No.43.pdf)). One guide recommends first sorting each narration into a family (UPI, NEFT, IMPS) by its leading tokens rather than writing dozens of brittle per-bank patterns ([terra-insight](https://www.terra-insight.com/insights/bank-statement-narration-pattern-classification-india/)). From there the proven order is: parse the narration, map the UPI ID (VPA) to a merchant (for example a Swiggy VPA becomes "Food delivery"), apply your own rules, look up previously confirmed transactions, and only then ask an LLM. Ask it with your category list as the only allowed answers and a few of your past corrections as examples. **actual-ai** shows the review half of this. It tags every guess `#actual-ai` and every failure `#actual-ai-miss` so you can check them, and it runs in dry-run mode by default ([GitHub](https://github.com/sakowicz/actual-ai)). The Firefly LLM categorizer shows the learning half: it **saves each AI decision as a rule** so the same merchant never costs another call ([Firefly-III-LLM-Categorizer](https://1clonegithubbyxjn.1934672944.workers.dev/constLiakos/Firefly-III-LLM-Categorizer)). One limit no model fixes: a UPI transfer to a person ("Rahul K") cannot be categorized from the narration. Your tool should ask you, or read the remark field.

Cost is small. No published per-month figures were found, but a rough estimate works. An individual has a few hundred transactions a month, rules and cache leave a few dozen for the LLM, and each call is a few hundred tokens. That comes to well under a million tokens a month: cents on a small cloud model, and nothing on a local model through Ollama (projects use Llama 3.1 8B ([actual-categorizer](https://github.com/annis-souames/actual-categorizer)) or Qwen 2.5 1.5B ([akkolluru](https://github.com/akkolluru/Expense-Tracker))). Check current pricing before relying on it.

**For the monthly advice, the rule is "code computes, the LLM narrates."** Finance-LLM research consistently keeps arithmetic away from the model. The CIFQA framework limits the LLM to understanding the question, planning and writing, with deterministic engines doing every calculation ([arXiv 2608.26114](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26114)). A 2026 benchmark found many LLMs wrongly flagged clean financial statements as inconsistent, so the model should not check its own work either ([arXiv 2605.29586](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29586)). In practice your code builds a "facts sheet" each month. It holds spending by category against last month and the 3-month average, top merchants, new or recurring subscriptions, unusually large items, savings rate, number of food-delivery orders, and the share still uncategorized. It treats **credit-card bill payments and transfers between your own accounts as transfers, not spending**, so nothing is counted twice, and counts SIPs and EMIs as committed outflows. The LLM gets only that sheet and writes three parts: what went well, where to improve, and one to three concrete actions. A final check pulls every ₹ figure and percentage out of the text and confirms each one appears in the facts sheet. If one doesn't, the text is regenerated or replaced by a plain template ([practitioner pipeline](https://wangari.substack.com/p/how-to-build-zero-hallucination-ai); [verification](https://aifinhub-monorepo.pages.dev/articles/hallucination-detection-at-scale-production/)). No open-source project publishes a tested monthly-review prompt, so this is the piece where your own work adds the most. Whether such advice actually changes spending has not been studied.

## The law barely touches a one-person tool, but security does

**India's DPDP Act does not apply to you if the tool is only for you.** Section 3(c)(i) excludes personal data processed by an individual for a personal or domestic purpose, and commentators read this as a full exclusion, not a partial exemption ([DPDP Wiki](https://dpdp.myndsolution.com/wiki/guides/who-does-the-act-apply-to/); [Sansa Legal](https://www.sansalegal.com/post/how-to-determine-the-application-of-the-dpdp-act-under-section-3)). Your statements contain other people's details (payee names, UPI IDs), but budgeting your own household is still a personal purpose. No court or Data Protection Board ruling has tested this yet. The picture changes the moment you process anyone else's data as a service, even for free. You then become a "Data Fiduciary" with duties for consent, security safeguards (Rule 6: encryption, masking, access control, logs, backups) and breach notification ([DPDP Wiki Rule 6](https://dpdp.myndsolution.com/wiki/rules/rule-6-reasonable-security-safeguards/)). Most of those duties take effect around **May 2027** ([TCSA](https://www.tcsa.in/resources/dpdp-rules-2025-implementation-roadmap)). Publishing the code for others to run on their own data likely keeps each user within the personal exclusion, but that is an inference, not settled law.

**RBI rules apply only to products.** Account Aggregator access needs FIU status, payment-data localisation applies to companies that run payment systems ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/article/news-ani/rbi-issues-clarifications-on-data-localisation-circular-119062700235_1.html)), and bill-fetch through BBPS goes through authorised operators. CRED itself started with email reading and, users report, moved card-due fetching to BBPS around mid-2024 ([Reddit](https://pol1.lr.ggtyler.dev/r/CreditCardsIndia/comments/1k2qyva/how_does_cred_get_access_to_statement_amount), anecdotal). None of this binds a personal tool. One thing to avoid even for personal use is **automated login to your own net banking**. No Indian rule addresses scraping your own account, but the IT Act's s.43 covers access "without permission", and courts have not settled whether a bank's terms define that permission ([law.asia](https://law.asia/india-data-scraping-regulation/)). The bigger risk is contractual: storing credentials or OTPs for a bot could weaken a fraud claim. That last point is an inference not verified against a specific bank's terms. Statements and alerts the bank sends you carry none of this risk.

**On LLM privacy**, both Anthropic and OpenAI say API data is not used for training by default. Both keep it for a limited period: OpenAI up to 30 days for abuse monitoring ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data)), and Anthropic reported as 7 days by some sources and 30 by others ([Anarlog](https://anarlog.so/blog/anthropic-data-retention-policy); [DevelopersDigest](https://www.developersdigest.tech/models/data-retention/anthropic)), so check the current policy. You can shrink exposure further. Send only the single transaction line, never the whole email. Mask card numbers to the last four digits, and strip account numbers, phone numbers, PAN/Aadhaar and OTPs first. For the monthly narrative, send only the aggregated facts sheet, which contains no account identifiers at all. Or run everything on a local model.

**Security is where a personal tool is most likely to go wrong.** Projects routinely keep PDF passwords in scripts or `.env` files ([xaneem](https://github.com/xaneem/hdfc-credit-card-statement-parser); [cshukla83](https://github.com/cshukla83/credit-card-tracker)). Do better. Keep the database encrypted with **SQLCipher**, with the key in your OS keychain, since SQLCipher deliberately stores no key itself ([Zetetic](https://www.zetetic.net/sqlcipher/database-key-material/)). Python can reach the keychain through `keyring` ([Charles Leifer](https://charlesleifer.com/blog/encrypted-sqlite-databases-with-python-and-sqlcipher/)). Keep the Gmail app password, PDF passwords and LLM key in the keychain too. Decrypt PDFs in memory only (the `indian-finance-parsers` package advertises this ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/indian-finance-parsers/))). Never log raw SMS or email bodies, git-ignore statement folders and database files, and back up only the encrypted database, with its key kept in a password manager.

## What a solo developer should actually build

**Build a small custom Python pipeline that runs once a month on statements, and borrow parsers rather than adopting a full app.** Your goal is a monthly report and advice, and that changes the whole design. Real-time capture is the hardest, most fragile and most privacy-sensitive part of every project above, and you don't need it. A month-end statement is complete (it includes the small UPI payments alerts miss), and you can check it with arithmetic, because the balances must reconcile. Here is how the options compare for your case:

| Option | Effort to start | Fit for monthly report + advice | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Custom pipeline (recommended)** | Medium: a parser per bank, plus SQLite and a report script | Exact fit; you own the facts sheet and prompt | **Do this** |
| Beancount + Fava as the store | Medium-high: learn double-entry | Strong; Indian importers and smart_importer exist; Fava gives free charts | Best "adopt" option if you like plain text |
| Firefly III + Data Importer | High: a Docker server with a database | Good rules/API, but you still build the report | Only if you also want a full web UI |
| Sure | Low to install | AI chat exists, but no monthly review; alpha churn | Watch it, don't depend on it |
| PennyWise AI on Android | Very low | Captures well, doesn't advise; export is Pro | Optional add-on for daily awareness |

The concrete build runs in three phases. **Phase one (manual, monthly)**: once a month, download each bank's CSV/XLS and each card's PDF into a dated folder. Run per-bank importers that reuse or adapt **akhilnarang/bank-statement-parser** and **xaneem's HDFC card parser** (both MIT), plus **casparser** if you want mutual funds. Every statement must pass the reconciliation check, or the run stops and tells you. Store rows in an encrypted SQLite database keyed on a unique ID (the UPI reference where it exists, otherwise a hash of date, amount and narration). Link card-bill payments and transfers between your own accounts so they are not counted as spending. Categorize in the layered order above: VPA/merchant map, your rules, past matches, then an LLM with your fixed category list. Anything the LLM decides goes into a short review list you clear in a few minutes, and each correction becomes a rule. Then compute the facts sheet, have the LLM write "what went well / where to improve / next month's actions" from it, check every number, and output a Markdown or HTML report, or email it to yourself. **Phase two (automated fetch)**: once the importers are stable, use IMAP with an app password and a Gmail label filter to pull statement PDFs automatically, with passwords from the keychain. **Phase three (optional, real-time)**: if you later want mid-month nudges, add a webhook that accepts SMS from an Android forwarder or an iOS Shortcut, or simply run PennyWise on your phone for live awareness, and let the statement remain the source of truth.

If you would rather adopt a store than design your own, **Beancount is the best base** for a Python developer who prioritizes reports. The ledger is a plain text file you can read, version and back up. Indian importers and smart_importer already exist. Fava gives you charts for free. Your monthly-advice script just queries the ledger. Firefly III is the choice if you later want a polished web app with a rules engine and API. Sure is the closest thing to "CRED-like" out of the box, but it is a fast-moving alpha whose AI features are a chat assistant, not a monthly review. Licensing barely matters for personal use: many of these projects are AGPL, which only bites if you distribute or host the software for others. Keep borrowed code to MIT libraries if you ever plan to share yours.

## Conclusion

The most useful shift in thinking is that a "CRED-like" tool for yourself is mainly **a reconciliation problem plus a writing problem, not a data-access problem**. Commercial apps chase real-time feeds and AA licences because they serve millions of users with no time to download statements. You have one user, a few accounts, and a monthly cadence, so the dull, complete, checkable statement beats every clever route. The open-source ecosystem has already solved most of the parsing in fragments. What it has not produced is a trustworthy monthly-advice layer, and that is where your effort pays off most: a facts sheet the code computes, a narrative the LLM writes, and a check that holds the LLM to the facts.

Two uncertainties are worth watching. A credit-card data type in Account Aggregator, or a "personal FIU" route, would change the ingestion picture, but there is no sign of either today. Bank statement formats will keep drifting, as SBI's July 2026 change showed. So the habit that decides whether the tool is still running in a year is not the choice of app. It is keeping a few real sample statements per bank as test files and letting the reconciliation check fail loudly the month a layout changes.
